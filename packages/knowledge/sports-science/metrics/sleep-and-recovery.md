# Sleep & Recovery

## Summary
Sleep is the single most potent recovery process the body has, and it is the
modifiable behaviour with the strongest, most consistent link to next-day
performance, reaction time, mood, and — in young athletes — injury risk. The
core actionable facts are conclusive: **acute sleep loss reliably degrades
endurance, power, speed, skill and perceived exertion** (meta-analysis of 45
studies), and **chronic restriction to ~5 h accumulates a "sleep debt" whose
neurobehavioural cost keeps growing with no adaptation** (Van Dongen 2003). The
coaching takeaway: protect 7–9 h habitually, treat a run of short nights as
accumulating fatigue (not a one-off), and bank extra sleep before hard blocks
and races. What is *less* settled is the size and reliability of the upside from
**sleep extension** — the famous Mah 2011 basketball study is real and
encouraging but is a small, uncontrolled single-arm trial, so the magnitude
should be held loosely. Recovery is multifactorial (sleep, nutrition,
psychological stress, training load) and sleep is necessary but not sufficient.

## What it is
**Sleep** is a reversible, regulated neurophysiological state organised into
repeating ~90-minute cycles, each passing through stages defined by EEG:

- **N1 / N2 (light NREM)** — the bulk of the night (~50%); N2 hosts sleep
  spindles linked to motor-memory consolidation.
- **N3 (slow-wave sleep, SWS, "deep sleep")** — the deepest NREM, dominant in
  the *first third* of the night. The period of greatest physical-restoration
  activity (growth-hormone pulse, anabolic tone).
- **REM (rapid-eye-movement)** — dominant in the *last third* of the night;
  associated with cognitive, emotional and skill/motor-learning consolidation.

Because SWS loads the early night and REM the late night, **a truncated night
preferentially steals REM**, and a fragmented night erodes both.

**Recovery** is the broader, multifactorial restoration of the
psychophysiological capacity to train again — driven by sleep, nutrition
(energy, protein, carbohydrate refuelling), psychological stress, and the
training load that created the fatigue in the first place. Sleep is the largest
single lever, but not the only one.

**Typical ranges.** General adult guidance is **7–9 h/night** (US National Sleep
Foundation). Athletes are often advised toward the upper end and sometimes 9–10 h
during heavy load, yet surveys consistently find **elite athletes habitually
under-sleep (<7 h) with poor efficiency** [Walsh 2021]. "Sleep need" is
individual; the practical target is the duration at which the runner wakes
unaided and is alert through the day.

## Physiology / mechanism
- **Anabolic / tissue repair.** The largest pulse of growth hormone is secreted
  during early-night slow-wave sleep; testosterone rises across the night.
  Together with sleep-associated shifts toward anabolism, this supports
  musculoskeletal repair, glycogen resynthesis and protein synthesis. Sleep
  restriction blunts these and shifts the milieu catabolic (↑ cortisol, ↓
  testosterone:cortisol).
- **Central / neurobehavioural.** The most reproducible cost of sleep loss is to
  the *brain*: vigilant attention, reaction time, executive function (working
  memory, inhibition, cognitive flexibility) and mood all degrade. This is the
  mechanism behind slower reaction time, more errors, poorer pacing decisions,
  and degraded skill control — and plausibly behind elevated injury risk.
- **Perceived effort.** Sleep loss raises the rating of perceived exertion (RPE)
  for a given workload, so the same pace *feels* harder — a key reason
  sleep-deprived endurance work feels disproportionately costly.
- **Immune & metabolic.** Short sleep impairs glucose handling, appetite
  regulation (↑ ghrelin, ↓ leptin) and immune function, plausibly raising
  illness risk during heavy training.
- **Homeostatic drive ("sleep debt").** Adenosine and other sleep-pressure
  signals accumulate with time awake; lost sleep is only partially "repaid" by
  later recovery sleep, and the deficit's cost compounds (Van Dongen 2003).

## The evidence

### Acute sleep loss degrades performance — Established
- **[Established]** Acute sleep deprivation significantly impairs physical
  performance across most domains — *meta-analysis of 45 studies (~670
  participants, athletes and non-athletes)* [Kong 2025]: standardized mean
  differences of **aerobic endurance −0.76**, **skill control −0.87**, **speed
  −0.58**, **explosive power −0.46**, **maximum force −0.24**, and **RPE +0.51**
  (effort feels harder). Effects were significant within the athlete-only
  subgroups. Findings are **consistent** with an earlier acute-sleep-loss review
  in *Sports Medicine* [Craven 2022], which likewise found small-to-moderate
  decrements that are larger for tasks done later in the day and for
  sustained/endurance tasks.
- **[Established]** **Anaerobic/short-sprint power is comparatively spared** —
  same meta-analysis [Kong 2025]: SMD −0.08 (non-significant). The deficit is
  concentrated in endurance, skill, and effort perception, not in brief maximal
  bursts. This is a *consistent* and important nuance: sleep loss hurts the
  aerobic, attention-demanding, and pacing-dependent parts of running most.
- **[Established]** Sleep loss prolongs **reaction time** and increases error
  rate and lapses of attention — *replicated across decades of lab studies* and
  reflected in the speed/skill-control decrements above. Reaction-time and
  vigilance decrements (e.g. via the Psychomotor Vigilance Task) are the
  best-replicated effect of sleep loss in all of sleep science.

### Chronic sleep restriction accumulates a debt — Established
- **[Established]** Moderate but sustained restriction produces **cumulative,
  dose-dependent neurobehavioural deficits with no adaptation** — *controlled
  laboratory dose-response trial, n=48 healthy adults* [Van Dongen 2003].
  Restriction to **6 h or 4 h time-in-bed for 14 nights** drove a steady,
  near-linear decline in vigilance and working memory; by 2 weeks the 6-h group
  performed comparably to people kept awake 1–2 full nights, and the 4-h group to
  2–3 nights of total deprivation. Crucially, **subjects underestimated their own
  impairment** — perceived sleepiness plateaued while objective performance kept
  falling. This is the foundational evidence that "I feel fine on 6 hours" is
  unreliable and that sleep debt is real and compounding.

### Sleep loss and injury risk in young athletes — Probable
- **[Probable]** In adolescents, chronically sleeping **<8 h/night is associated
  with ~1.7× the odds of musculoskeletal injury** — *retrospective cohort, 112
  middle/high-school athletes aged 12–18, injuries tracked over 21 months*
  [Milewski 2014]. Hours of sleep was the strongest injury predictor in
  univariate analysis and remained significant alongside school grade in
  multivariate analysis. A *systematic review/meta-analysis* of adolescent
  athletes later reported a consistent direction of effect [Gao 2019].
  **Caveats:** the design is observational and retrospective (recall/confounding
  — school grade, training volume, and chronological age all track together), and
  the population is *adolescents*, so the magnitude should not be assumed
  identical in adult recreational runners. The *direction* (less sleep → more
  injury) is consistent; the *quantified odds ratio* is specific to this cohort.

### Sleep extension benefits — Emerging
- **[Emerging]** Extending sleep may improve performance, reaction time and mood —
  *small, uncontrolled, single-arm trial, n=11 male collegiate basketball
  players* [Mah 2011]. After 5–7 weeks of targeting ~10 h in bed (adding ~111
  min/night vs baseline), players ran a timed sprint faster (16.2→15.5 s),
  improved free-throw and 3-point accuracy by ~9%, and showed **faster reaction
  time** and better mood. **Why only Emerging:** no control group, tiny
  basketball-specific sample, possible practice/learning and seasonal effects,
  and the athletes started sleep-deficient (so part of the gain is *correcting a
  deficit* rather than a benefit of "extra" sleep in an already-rested person).
  Subsequent expert consensus likewise notes the sleep-extension literature is
  small and heterogeneous and that effects vary by the variable measured and
  should be interpreted with caution [Walsh 2021]. So: extending sleep in an
  under-slept runner is very likely beneficial; the *size* of the gain and any
  benefit of sleeping beyond one's actual need are not established.

### Recovery is multifactorial — Probable / consensus
- **[Probable]** Recovery from training is multifactorial and best monitored and
  managed across sleep, nutrition, psychological stress and load — *expert
  consensus statement* [Kellmann 2018]. Under-recovery is a precursor to
  functional overreaching and overtraining; sleep is the dominant recovery lever
  but does not substitute for adequate fuelling or stress management. Treat this
  as well-reasoned consensus rather than a single quantified trial.
- **[Probable]** Athletes are a *high-risk* group for inadequate sleep — *expert
  consensus / narrative review* [Walsh 2021]: habitual short and fragmented
  sleep is common (travel, early sessions, evening competition, screen/arousal),
  and the consensus recommends prioritising sleep duration and hygiene, with
  individualised napping and pre-travel banking.

## How we compute it
Daud derives sleep signals from wearable/phone sleep tracking (or manual logs):

- `sleepDurationNightly` — total sleep time per night (h). Wearable estimates of
  *total sleep* are reasonably good; **stage estimates (REM/deep/light) from
  consumer wrist devices are weak** versus polysomnography and should be treated
  as low-confidence trend data, not ground truth.
- `sleepDebt7d` — rolling shortfall vs the runner's personal target over 7 days:
  `Σ max(0, target − actual)` across the last 7 nights (h). A simple, transparent
  proxy for the Van Dongen "debt" concept; it is a heuristic, not a validated
  biomarker.
- Recovery is **not** reduced to a single number — sleep is read *alongside*
  morning HRV/RHR, subjective wellness, and acute:chronic load (see
  `heart-rate-variability`, `resting-heart-rate`, `training-load-acwr`).

Ground-truth caveat: true sleep architecture requires PSG. Everything here from a
wearable is an estimate; the coach weights **duration trends** far more than
device "deep sleep minutes."

## How the coach uses it
Decision logic, scaled by stage (Stage 1 beginner → Stage 3 racing):

- **Target.** Default personal target 7–9 h (the NSF figure this note cites; #98);
  nudge toward the upper end during
  heavy blocks and taper. The operative target is *individualised* from the
  runner's own alert-without-alarm duration, not a fixed number.
- **One bad night.** A single short/poor night → keep the easy day, but **reduce
  or postpone a planned hard/quality session** if the night was severe (e.g.
  <60% of target), because sleep loss raises RPE and degrades skill/pacing.
  Don't over-react to isolated noise.
- **Accumulating debt.** If `sleepDebt7d` is rising or several nights run short,
  treat fatigue as **cumulative** (Van Dongen): lower intensity first, then
  volume, and flag elevated injury/illness vulnerability — especially for
  younger athletes (Milewski).
- **Before key sessions/races.** Encourage **banking sleep** in the nights
  *before* (sleep extension/taper sleep), since the night *before* a race is
  often poor from nerves; the cushion is the prior nights.
- **Cross-checks.** Read sleep with HRV/RHR and wellness. Converging signals
  (short sleep + suppressed HRV + high RPE + sore) = strong case to back off; a
  lone soft signal = watch, don't panic.
- **Stage differences.** Stage 1: focus on consistency/habit and basic hygiene,
  not metrics. Stage 2: use sleep-debt trends to time quality work. Stage 3:
  protect pre-competition sleep, manage travel/time-zones, individualise napping.

## Honesty & uncertainty
- **Causality vs correlation.** The injury and many performance links in the
  field are *observational*; sleep travels with training load, life stress, age
  and nutrition, which independently affect outcomes. Acute lab deprivation
  studies establish causation for *performance*, but use extreme protocols (often
  total or severe partial deprivation) that exceed what most runners experience.
- **Sleep extension magnitude is unsettled.** The headline numbers (Mah 2011) come
  from one tiny, uncontrolled study; the benefit of sleep *beyond* an individual's
  need — as opposed to *correcting a deficit* — is not demonstrated. Hold the
  effect size loosely.
- **Consumer stage data is unreliable.** Wrist-wearable REM/deep-sleep figures
  diverge substantially from PSG. Don't coach off device stage percentages; use
  duration trends and subjective rest.
- **Individual variation is large.** True sleep need varies genetically; a rare
  minority function on less, but self-assessed tolerance is *systematically
  optimistic* (Van Dongen) — people underestimate their own impairment.
- **Necessary, not sufficient.** Good sleep does not offset poor fuelling,
  excessive load, or chronic life stress. Recovery is multifactorial; sleep is
  the biggest single lever, not the only one.
- **Direction is robust even where magnitude isn't.** Across all the above, the
  *sign* of the effect (more/better sleep → better recovery and performance, less
  → worse) is highly consistent; the precise effect sizes and thresholds are
  where the genuine uncertainty lies.

## Safety bounds
- **No hard physiological guardrail** is breached by sleep alone, but the coach
  must **never prescribe or encourage sleep restriction** to "fit in" training,
  and must treat persistent severe short sleep as a flag to *reduce* load, not
  add it.
- For **adolescent/youth** runners, under-8-h habitual sleep is an explicit
  injury-risk flag (Milewski) and should bias the plan conservative.
- Sudden, unexplained collapse in sleep quality alongside rising RHR, low mood
  and poor performance can indicate **overreaching/overtraining or illness** —
  escalate to a deload and, if persistent, recommend medical/professional review.
- **Partly enforced in code — one bound of the three, and only one direction of it.**
  "Never prescribe or encourage sleep restriction" IS a deterministic block: the
  hand-compiled rule `advise_sleep_restriction` in
  `apps/server/src/healthee/insights/output_guard.py` names this note's *Safety bounds*
  bullet (`sleep-and-recovery.md:233`) as one of its origins and refuses to ship an
  answer that advises cutting sleep to fit training. The deload/medical-review
  escalation and the youth-sleep flag are **not** enforced anywhere — they are rules for
  the coach to follow, not guarantees. (The

## Bottom line

**Act on confidently (conclusive):**
- Acute sleep loss reliably degrades endurance, speed, power, skill control and
  reaction time, and raises perceived effort — endurance and skill suffer most;
  brief anaerobic power is largely spared [Kong 2025, Craven 2022].
- Sleep debt is real, cumulative and dose-dependent, with no adaptation and with
  people underestimating their own impairment [Van Dongen 2003]. A run of short
  nights is accumulating fatigue, not a series of independent events.
- 7–9 h is the sensible habitual target; chronically short sleep is a genuine
  performance and (in youth) injury liability [Milewski 2014, Walsh 2021].
- Recovery is multifactorial — sleep is necessary but not sufficient [Kellmann
  2018].

**Hold loosely (unsettled):**
- The *magnitude* of sleep-extension benefits, and whether sleeping beyond one's
  actual need helps an already-rested runner (Mah 2011 is small and uncontrolled).
- The precise injury odds-ratio outside adolescents (Milewski is observational,
  retrospective, youth-specific).
- Consumer-wearable REM/deep-sleep stage numbers as a coaching signal.
- Exact individual sleep-need thresholds — individualise from the runner's data.

## Coach Directives
- **D1:** Set each runner a personal nightly sleep target (**default 7–9 h**);
  individualise from their own alert-without-alarm duration over time. *(The default
  read "7.5–9 h" here and under "How the coach uses it", while the note's evidence
  section, its summary and its Bottom line all say **7–9 h** — the sourced US
  National Sleep Foundation figure. The 7.5 lower bound had no source and no
  explanation; it is aligned to the cited one, #98.)* — confidence: Established
- **D2:** Never prescribe, endorse, or design plans that require habitual sleep
  restriction to accommodate training. — confidence: Established (safety)
- **D3:** After a single severely short night (<~60% of target), keep easy work
  but downgrade or postpone planned hard/quality sessions; expect inflated RPE.
  Do not over-react to isolated noise. — confidence: Established that a severely short
  night degrades next-day quality work; **the <~60%-of-target trigger is unsourced** practitioner/methodological convention — no source in this note supports it (#100).
- **D4:** Track rolling 7-day sleep debt; if it rises or several nights run short,
  treat fatigue as cumulative — cut intensity first, then volume — and raise
  injury/illness vigilance. — confidence: Established
- **D5:** For adolescent/youth runners, flag habitual <8 h sleep as an injury-risk
  factor and bias the plan conservative. — confidence: Probable
- **D6:** Encourage banking sleep (extension) in the nights *before* key sessions
  and races; don't rely on the night immediately before. — confidence: Probable
  (benefit direction Established; magnitude Emerging)
- **D7:** Read sleep alongside HRV, RHR, RPE and subjective wellness; act
  decisively only on converging signals, watch on a lone one. — confidence:
  Probable
- **D8:** Do not coach off consumer-wearable sleep-stage percentages; use total
  duration trends and subjective rest. — confidence: Probable
- **D9:** Treat recovery as multifactorial — pair sleep guidance with fuelling and
  stress management; never imply sleep alone offsets under-fuelling or overload. —
